Do I Need Workers' Comp Insurance for 1 Employee?

In 47 of 50 US states, the answer is yes — from your very first W-2 hire, even if they're part-time, seasonal, or working just a few hours a week. This guide breaks down the state rules, exemptions, penalties for going uncovered, and what it actually costs.

By Emiliano · Last updated 2026-10-13 · 9 min read

The short answer

Once you hire your first W-2 employee in almost every US state, workers' compensation insurance is legally required. This applies to:

  • Part-time employees
  • Seasonal workers
  • Family members employed by the business
  • Corporate officers in many states (unless exempted by election)

The coverage is mandatory from the first day of employment in most states. There's no grace period and no minimum-hours threshold.

State-by-state rules

Three states have exemptions or thresholds worth knowing:

StateRule for 1st Employee
TexasNo mandate. Workers' comp is voluntary for private employers.
South CarolinaRequired at 4+ employees in most industries; 1+ in construction and manufacturing.
New JerseyRequired for most industries with 1+ employees. Some exemptions for nonprofit and agricultural employers.
All other 47 states + DCRequired from 1st W-2 employee (part-time included).

Your state department of labor or insurance is the authoritative source. If you're unsure, assume you need it — the penalties are much worse than the premium.

What happens if you don't have it

  • Fines: $1,000–$10,000 per violation, plus $100–$1,000 per day of non-compliance in some states
  • Personal liability: You become personally responsible for the full cost of any workplace injury — medical, lost wages, disability, death benefits. This is unlimited.
  • Civil lawsuits: In many states, employees can sue you in civil court for negligence
  • Business shutdown: Some states will issue a stop-work order until coverage is in place
  • Criminal charges: In extreme cases, some states pursue misdemeanor or felony charges

None of these outcomes are worth saving a few hundred dollars on a premium.

Exceptions and edge cases

Sole proprietors and partners

Sole proprietors, general partners, and most LLC members are exempt from workers' comp for themselves. They can elect to be included, but it's not required. This exemption does not extend to any W-2 employees the business hires.

Independent contractors (1099)

If a worker is truly independent, you do not need to cover them under your workers' comp. But state agencies can reclassify 1099 workers as employees, making you retroactively liable. Consult a CPA before relying on 1099 classification.

Corporate officers

Rules vary widely. Some states require officers to be covered, some allow exemption by election, some treat officers as employees automatically.

Family members

Many states exempt family members from mandatory coverage, but the rules are inconsistent.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need workers' comp for 1 employee?

In 47 of 50 US states, workers' compensation insurance is mandatory as soon as you hire your first W-2 employee, even if it's part-time or seasonal. The three exceptions with threshold requirements are Texas, South Carolina, and New Jersey.

What happens if I don't have workers' comp for my one employee?

Penalties range from $1,000–$10,000 per violation, plus you can be personally liable for the full cost of any workplace injury. In some states, employees can sue you in civil court for negligence. Some states also shut down your business until coverage is in place.

How much does workers' comp cost for 1 employee?

For a clerical employee earning $40,000 per year, workers' comp costs roughly $200 in North Dakota to $1,010 in Hawaii. High-risk class codes pay much more: a roofer with the same payroll pays $2,000–$4,800 depending on state.

Do I need workers' comp for a 1099 contractor?

Generally no, but state rules vary. If the contractor is truly independent, they are typically not covered by your workers' comp. But if the state reclassifies them as an employee, you could be liable retroactively.

Do sole proprietors need workers' comp for themselves?

No. Sole proprietors, partners, and LLC members are typically exempt from workers' comp for themselves. This exemption applies to the business owner — not to any W-2 employee the business hires.
