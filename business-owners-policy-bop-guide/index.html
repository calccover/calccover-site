Business Owner's Policy (BOP): What It Covers, Who Needs It, and What It Costs

A Business Owner's Policy is the most efficient way for a small business to buy insurance. It bundles general liability and commercial property into one policy at a discounted rate — typically saving 10–25% versus buying them separately. This guide explains exactly what a BOP covers, what it doesn't, who qualifies, and what it should cost in 2026.

What is a Business Owner's Policy?

A Business Owner's Policy (BOP) is a bundled insurance package designed specifically for small and medium-sized businesses. It combines two core coverages — general liability and commercial property — into a single policy, and usually adds business income and extra expense coverage at no additional cost.

BOPs are standardized products. That's what makes them cheap. Insurance carriers pre-underwrite the two coverages, set standard limits, and offer the bundle at a discount versus standalone policies. The tradeoff is flexibility: a BOP has eligibility restrictions (revenue, industry, location) and limited customization compared to a Commercial Package Policy.

For most small, low-risk businesses — retail, offices, restaurants, salons, light manufacturing, professional services — a BOP is the right starting point. It's the cheapest way to get the two coverages every business needs, plus business interruption coverage that standalone GL doesn't include.

The two core coverages inside a BOP

General liability covers third-party claims of bodily injury, property damage, and personal/advertising injury caused by your business operations. If a customer slips on your floor, a delivery person is injured on your loading dock, or a competitor claims you ran a misleading ad, GL pays defense costs and damages up to your limit.

Commercial property covers your building (if you own it), your business personal property (equipment, furniture, inventory, tenant improvements), and — with certain endorsements — valuable papers, electronic data processing equipment, and signs. Property coverage pays to repair or replace damaged assets, subject to your deductible.

Business income and extra expense — included automatically in almost every BOP — covers lost revenue and ongoing expenses if a covered property loss forces you to close or relocate. Without it, a single fire or storm that closes your business for 60 days would leave you paying rent, salaries, and loan payments with no income. That coverage is a major reason BOPs are more valuable than the sum of their parts.

What a BOP covers (in detail)

Standard BOP coverage includes:

General liability

  • Bodily injury — injuries to customers, vendors, and visitors on your premises or at job sites
  • Property damage — damage your business causes to third-party property
  • Personal and advertising injury — libel, slander, false arrest, invasion of privacy, copyright infringement in advertising
  • Products and completed operations — claims arising from products you sell or work you completed after the job is done
  • Legal defense costs — attorney fees and court costs, paid in addition to the liability limit
  • Medical payments — small medical bills regardless of fault (typically $5,000-$10,000)

Commercial property

  • Buildings — if you own your commercial location
  • Business personal property — furniture, fixtures, equipment, inventory, supplies
  • Tenant improvements — improvements you made to a leased space
  • Signs, fences, and outdoor property — subject to sublimits
  • Valuable papers and records — up to a sublimit, often $5,000-$25,000
  • Electronic data processing equipment — computers and servers, often requiring an endorsement

Business income and extra expense

  • Lost net income — profit you would have earned during a covered shutdown
  • Continuing operating expenses — rent, utilities, loan payments, key employee salaries
  • Extra expense — costs of operating from a temporary location
  • Civil authority actions — losses when a government order prevents access to your business
Standard BOP limits: Most BOPs default to $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate on the liability side, and $500K-$1M on the property side. Higher limits are available for additional premium.

For most small businesses, the priority stack is: BOP first, then workers' comp (if you have employees), then any industry-specific coverage (E&O, liquor, cyber, commercial auto) required by your operations, contracts, or state law.

Who qualifies for a BOP?

Each carrier sets its own eligibility rules, but most BOPs require the following:

  • Revenue under $5M — some carriers cap at $3M, others at $10M
  • Fewer than 100 employees — some cap at 50
  • Low-to-medium risk class code — no roofing, trucking, bars, heavy manufacturing
  • Commercial location — home-based businesses are usually excluded
  • Building value under $1M-$2M — higher-value properties go to CPP
  • Less than 4 stories tall — for property coverage
  • No more than one commercial location — some carriers allow multiple

Industries that commonly qualify: retail stores, restaurants (without bars), offices, professional services, salons, fitness studios, medical and dental offices, contractors (small, low-risk), auto repair shops, event planners, and most B2B service businesses.

Industries that typically do NOT qualify: roofing, trucking, waste hauling, bars and nightclubs, heavy manufacturing, oil and gas, construction above a certain revenue, and any business with significant pollution exposure.

National average BOP premium: roughly $1,400 per year for a small business with under $5M in revenue and a clean claims history. That's the total across GL, property, and business income — often $300-$500 per year cheaper than buying GL and property as standalone policies.

What drives BOP cost up

  • Revenue above $1M — premium scales roughly linearly with revenue
  • Customer foot traffic — restaurants and retail pay more than offices
  • Building age and construction type — older buildings cost more to insure
  • State and city — Northeast and coastal states cost 40–60% more
  • Higher property values — every $100K in insured value adds $200-$500 in annual premium
  • Prior claims — a single GL claim can raise the premium 15–40% for three years

What drives BOP cost down

  • Higher deductibles — $1,000 to $2,500 on GL saves 10–15%
  • Newer building — post-2000 construction costs 15–25% less to insure
  • Low-crime location — suburban and rural rates are lower
  • Clean claims history — 3+ years with no claims qualifies for preferred pricing
  • Annual pay — paying upfront saves 3–8% versus monthly installments

Three ways to buy GL and property coverage, each with different tradeoffs:

When a BOP is the right answer: 90% of small businesses under $5M revenue, low-to-medium risk industry, commercial location, standard coverage needs.

When a CPP is better: higher-risk industries (roofing, trucking, manufacturing), larger businesses, businesses needing auto + property + GL + inland marine in one policy, or businesses that want very specific coverage customization.

When standalone is better: when one coverage dominates (e.g., a business that needs $5M in property coverage but minimal GL), or when you want to work with different carriers for GL and property for strategic reasons.

How to buy a BOP

  1. Confirm eligibility. Ask a carrier or broker: does my industry, revenue, and location qualify? If not, you need a CPP instead.
  2. Get 3+ quotes. Identical coverage varies 20–30% between carriers. Loyalty to one carrier is expensive.
  3. Set the right liability limits. Default $1M/$2M works for most small businesses. Increase to $2M/$4M if contracts or leases require it, or if your liability exposure is high.
  4. Set the right property limit. Insure to replacement cost, not actual cash value, unless you have a specific reason. Underinsuring property is the most common BOP mistake.
  5. Choose a deductible. $1,000 is standard. $2,500 saves 10–15% and is manageable for most businesses.
  6. Confirm business income sublimit. Standard is 12 months of lost income. If your business would take longer than 12 months to recover from a fire, increase the sublimit.
  7. Read the exclusions. Note what you need to add separately (workers' comp, E&O, cyber, commercial auto).
  8. Bind and document. Get a Certificate of Insurance to send to landlords and clients that require proof.
Pro tip: Ask your broker to confirm your NCCI class code in writing before quoting. Misclassification is the #1 reason small businesses overpay on a BOP — sometimes by 30–50%.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Business Owner's Policy (BOP)?

A Business Owner's Policy is a bundled insurance package that combines general liability and commercial property coverage into a single policy for small, low-risk businesses. It typically saves 10–25% versus buying the two coverages separately, and most BOPs include business income and extra expense coverage automatically.

How much does a BOP cost?

A BOP for a small business with under $5M in revenue and a low-risk industry typically costs $600–$2,500 per year, with a national average around $1,400. Restaurants, contractors, and salons pay more. Retail, offices, and professional services pay less. State variation swings the total 40–60%.

Who qualifies for a BOP?

Eligibility varies by carrier, but most BOPs require: revenue under $5M (some carriers cap at $3M), under 100 employees, a low-to-medium risk industry class, a commercial location (not home-based), and building value under $1M–$2M. High-risk industries (roofing, trucking, bars) are excluded from BOPs and need a Commercial Package Policy instead.

What does a BOP not cover?

A standard BOP does NOT cover: professional liability / errors & omissions, workers' compensation, commercial auto, cyber liability, employment practices liability (EPLI), liquor liability, or pollution. These require separate policies. A BOP is the foundation, not the entire insurance program for most businesses.

Is a BOP better than buying GL and property separately?

For most small, low-risk businesses, yes. A BOP saves 10–25% versus standalone policies, includes business income coverage automatically, and simplifies administration (one policy, one renewal date, one deductible per occurrence). Standalone policies are better when your property needs are unusual, when you need very high property limits, or when you want different carriers for GL and property for strategic reasons.

What is the difference between a BOP and a CPP?

A BOP is a standardized bundle with eligibility restrictions, designed for small low-risk businesses. A Commercial Package Policy (CPP) is a more flexible bundle that can include GL, property, commercial auto, inland marine, crime, and other coverages, and it can be written for any industry or size. Larger or higher-risk businesses use CPPs. Small businesses almost always start with a BOP.

Can I get a BOP for a home-based business?

Standard BOPs require a commercial location, so most home-based businesses do not qualify. Instead, look for a home-based business insurance policy or an in-home business endorsement on a homeowners policy. Some carriers offer modified BOPs for home-based businesses with lower property limits and no customer foot traffic exposure.

How much general liability coverage does a BOP include?

Most BOPs default to $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate for general liability. Higher limits are available ($2M/$4M, $3M/$6M) for an additional premium. Commercial leases and B2B contracts typically require at least $1M/$2M, so the default limit works for most businesses. Businesses with high liability exposure should add an umbrella policy on top of the BOP.

Does a BOP cover business interruption?

Yes. Business income and extra expense coverage is included automatically in almost every BOP. It covers lost income and ongoing expenses if your business is forced to close due to a covered property loss (fire, storm, etc.). Coverage typically has a 12-month limit and a 72-hour waiting period. This is one of the biggest value-adds of a BOP versus standalone property insurance.

How do I know if my business qualifies for a BOP?

Ask any BOP carrier three questions: (1) Do you write my industry class code? (2) Do you accept my revenue level? (3) Do you cover businesses at my location? If the answer is yes to all three, you likely qualify. If your business is high-risk (roofing, trucking, bars, manufacturing), you will need a Commercial Package Policy instead.
