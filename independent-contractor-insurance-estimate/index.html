This guide breaks down exactly what you should pay by trade, what coverages you actually need, and how to lower your premium without cutting corners.

What coverages you actually need

Independent contractors buy a specific set of coverages. Here's the priority list:

General Liability (always)

Protects against third-party bodily injury and property damage caused by your work. If you damage a client's property, injure someone at a job site, or cause a fire that spreads, GL pays. Most commercial contracts, general contractors, and property managers require proof of $1M/$2M before allowing you on site.

Typical cost: $400–$1,500 per year. Calculate your GL →

Professional Liability / E&O (if you provide advice or design)

Covers claims that your advice, designs, or work product caused a client financial loss. Distinct from GL, which covers physical injury — E&O covers mistakes in the quality of your work. Consultants, engineers, IT professionals, designers, and architects need it. Licensed tradespeople (some plumbers, electricians) may also need it for code-compliance claims.

Typical cost: $500–$1,800 per year. Calculate your E&O →

Commercial Auto (if you drive to job sites)

Personal auto policies exclude business use. If you drive to client sites, transport tools, or use a vehicle for any business purpose, you need commercial auto. Even "just visiting clients" counts.

Typical cost: $1,400–$4,500 per vehicle per year. Calculate your commercial auto →

Workers' Comp (if you have employees)

Mandatory in almost every state once you hire your first W-2 employee, part-time included. Rates vary from $1.50 to $12.00 per $100 of payroll depending on trade. If you misclassify employees as 1099, you could be retroactively liable.

Typical cost: $1,500–$5,000 per year for a small crew. Calculate your workers' comp →

Inland Marine (if you own tools and equipment)

Covers tools and equipment in transit between job sites. Standard commercial property insurance excludes off-premises equipment — which is where most contractor tools live. Inland marine closes that gap.

Typical cost: $200–$800 per year. Calculate your inland marine →

Commercial Umbrella (if contracts require higher limits)

Adds excess liability on top of your GL and auto. Cheap ($200–$800 per year for $1M) and recommended for any contractor whose contracts require limits above $1M/$2M.

Typical cost: $200–$800 per year. Calculate your umbrella →

BOP Bundle (best value for most)

A Business Owner's Policy bundles GL and property (and often business income) into one policy at a 10–25% discount. If you qualify (low-risk trade, under $5M revenue, commercial location), it's the cheapest way to get your core coverages.

Typical cost: $600–$2,500 per year. Calculate your BOP → or read our complete BOP guide.

What drives your premium up or down

Factors that raise your premium

  • High-risk trade — roofing, excavation, and heavy construction pay 5–15x more than consulting
  • State and metro area — Northeast and coastal states cost 40–60% more than Mountain West
  • Revenue above $100K — scales roughly linearly with revenue
  • Employees on payroll — workers' comp adds $1,500–$5,000+ per year
  • Claims history — one claim raises premiums 15–40% for 3 years
  • Higher coverage limits — $2M/$4M costs 20–35% more than $1M/$2M
  • Subcontractor use — if you hire subs, you need to verify their insurance or you may be liable for their claims

Factors that lower your premium

  • Clean 3-year claims history — qualifies for preferred pricing tiers
  • Safety program documentation — formal training reduces workers' comp 5–15%
  • Higher deductibles — $500 to $2,500 on GL saves 10–15%
  • Bundling coverage — BOP or package policy saves 10–25%
  • Annual pay instead of monthly — avoids 3–8% installment fees
  • Shop 3+ carriers every year — rates for identical coverage vary 20–30%

Common mistakes independent contractors make

  1. Assuming the hiring company covers you. General contractors, property managers, and clients do not extend their insurance to you. You are a separate business and need your own coverage.
  2. Using personal auto for work. If you get in an accident driving to a job site, your personal auto policy can deny the claim. This is one of the most common — and most expensive — mistakes.
  3. Skipping workers' comp because subs are 1099. If your state reclassifies them as employees, you're retroactively liable. California AB5, New Jersey, and other states have strict classification tests.
  4. Buying the cheapest GL you can find. The cheapest policy often excludes the specific work you do. Read the exclusions before you bind.
  5. Not updating coverage as you grow. Adding employees, buying equipment, or expanding into new services requires coverage updates. Underinsuring after a growth event is the most common reason contractors get burned.
  6. Not getting a Certificate of Insurance (COI). Landlords, clients, and general contractors require a COI before they work with you. Not having one can cost you jobs. See our complete COI guide.

How to buy independent contractor insurance

  1. Confirm your class code. Ask any broker to confirm your NCCI class code in writing. Misclassification is the #1 reason contractors overpay — sometimes by 30–50%.
  2. Get 3+ quotes. Rates for identical coverage vary 20–30% between carriers. Loyalty is expensive.
  3. Verify AM Best rating. A- or better. This matters at claim time.
  4. Read the exclusions. Every policy has them. Note what's excluded — particularly the specific work you do.
  5. Set the right deductible. $1,000 is standard; $2,500 saves 10–15%.
  6. Get a COI. Request one after binding. Send it to every landlord, general contractor, and client who asks.
  7. Review annually. Your coverage needs change as you grow. Underinsuring after a growth event is the most common and expensive mistake contractors make.

Frequently asked questions

How much does independent contractor insurance cost?

Short answer: Independent contractor insurance costs $400–$1,500 per year for general liability ($1M/$2M), which is the core coverage most contractors need. If you have employees, workers' comp adds $1,500–$5,000 per year depending on trade and state. If you own tools and equipment, inland marine adds $200–$800 per year.

Most independent contractors spend $600–$2,500 per year total on the coverages they actually need.

What insurance does an independent contractor need?

Short answer: Short answer: Most independent contractors need three core coverages: (1) general liability for third-party injuries and property damage at client sites, (2) professional liability / errors & omissions if you provide advice or design work, and (3) commercial auto if you drive to job sites.

If you have employees, workers' comp is mandatory in almost every state.

If you own tools and equipment that travel with you, inland marine covers them.

Do 1099 independent contractors need workers' comp?

Short answer: No, 1099 independent contractors are typically not eligible for workers' comp through the hiring company, and you are not legally required to carry workers' comp for yourself in most states. However, if you hire W-2 employees or sub-contractors you misclassified as 1099, workers' comp becomes mandatory.

Some states (like California under AB5) have strict classification tests that can reclassify 1099 workers as employees, making the hiring company retroactively liable for workers' comp.

Is independent contractor insurance required by law?

Short answer: General liability is not legally mandated, but most commercial contracts, general contractors, and property managers require proof of $1M/$2M general liability before they will work with you. Commercial auto is legally required for any vehicle used for business. Workers' comp is mandatory if you have employees.

Professional liability is often required by state licensing boards for engineers, architects, and certain trades.

What is the cheapest insurance for independent contractors?

Short answer: A standalone general liability policy is the cheapest option for most independent contractors, averaging $30–$80 per month for a $1M/$2M policy in a low-risk trade. If you need both GL and property coverage, a Business Owner's Policy (BOP) bundling them saves 10–25% versus buying separately.

Direct-to-business carriers like Hiscox, Next Insurance, and Thimble offer the lowest rates for simple risks.

How do I estimate my contractor insurance premium?

Short answer: Your premium is driven by: (1) trade classification — electrical and roofing pay 3–5x more than consulting or cleaning; (2) annual revenue — scales linearly above $100K; (3) state — Northeast and coastal states cost 40–60% more than Mountain West; (4) payroll if you have employees; (5) claims history — one claim can raise premiums 15–40% for 3 years; (6) coverage limits — $2M costs 20–35% more than $1M.

Do I need insurance as a solo independent contractor?

Short answer: Yes. Even a solo contractor with no employees needs general liability if they visit client sites, professional liability if they provide advice or design work, and commercial auto if they drive to jobs. Personal auto and homeowners policies exclude business use, so without commercial coverage you are personally liable for any claim.

Most solo contractors spend $500–$1,500 per year total.
